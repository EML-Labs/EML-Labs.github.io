Research overview

Predicting AF from the timing between heartbeats

From a global health problem to a deployable, patient-aware machine learning framework: how the pieces fit together.

Background

Why paroxysmal AF, and why intervals?

Atrial fibrillation is the most common sustained heart-rhythm disorder, affecting more than 37 million people and raising stroke risk roughly fivefold. Its earliest, most treatable form, paroxysmal AF, starts and stops on its own, is often symptomless, and is easily missed by brief clinical tests.

AF reveals itself most clearly in the RR-interval sequence: the gaps between consecutive heartbeats. Both single-lead ECG and the PPG optical sensor in everyday smartwatches capture this same beat-to-beat timing, making continuous, low-cost screening realistic, especially in settings where clinical-grade monitoring is out of reach.

Challenges

Four barriers to real-world deployment

1 · Cross-patient generalisation

Supervised models trained on RR dynamics perform well on average but collapse on patients outside the training distribution; sensitivity has been reported to fall from ~99% to ~86% on fully held-out patients.

2 · Representation under variability

Standard contrastive methods treat all same-class samples as interchangeable, folding each patient's distinct physiology into one shared cluster; the very structure needed for transfer is discarded.

3 · Subtle pre-onset precursors

The autonomic signs that precede an episode (rising vagal tone, increasing ectopic burden) are faint, individual, and unfold against each patient's own shifting baseline.

4 · Computational constraints

Wearables impose strict limits on model size, memory, and latency. Many high-performing AF models are built for server-grade infrastructure and simply cannot run on-device.

Objectives

What the project set out to do

  • Characterise the geometry of the learned embedding under different contrastive objectives and explain why standard ones fail to generalise.
  • Design a patient-aware contrastive objective that preserves per-patient rhythm structure while separating AF from sinus rhythm.
  • Build a lightweight multi-branch encoder that runs on wearable hardware and reads both ECG and PPG intervals.
  • Evaluate under a strict patient-independent protocol against state-of-the-art interval-based methods.
  • Develop a per-patient early-warning score for AF onset, calibrated from a single labelled episode.

Methodology

The pipeline, end to end

From raw intervals on the wrist to a calibrated, patient-specific warning score.

1 · Signal & preprocessing

RR intervals (ECG) and pulse intervals (PPG) are segmented into fixed windows and cleaned, a single pipeline for either modality.

2 · Patient-aware pretraining

The encoder learns representations with positive pairs limited to same-patient, same-class segments, preserving individual baselines.

3 · Lightweight probe

A small linear classifier on top of the frozen embedding detects AF with only a handful of labels, cheap to train and to run.

4 · Geometry analysis

We quantify per-patient cohesion and class separability to explain, not just measure, why the approach transfers.

5 · Personal early warning

A personal sinus-to-AF axis is calibrated per patient; later segments are scored by how far they drift along it.

6 · Zero-shot validation

The frozen encoder is tested on an entirely separate dataset (SHDB-AF) with no fine-tuning, probing true robustness.

Key insight

The “BCE paradox”

Binary cross-entropy training produces the cleanest global class separation, yet the most disordered per-patient structure and the worst transfer to unseen patients. Clean global separation is a misleading indicator; per-subject geometric consistency is the real predictor of robust generalisation.
